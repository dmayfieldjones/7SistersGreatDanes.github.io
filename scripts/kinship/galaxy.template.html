    Methods behind the graphic

    The data

    The starting point is a pedigree export of dogs, with sire, dam, birth date, coat color and registered name. We added our own dogs and litters from our litter pages, along with their ancestors, so Piper, Mia and their puppies connect to the rest of the breed. The page carries only anonymous fields for each dog: its position, a birth year, a coat family, a 10-generation inbreeding value, its sire and dam as row numbers in a shuffled order, and which kennel names it carries. It holds no dog names or registration numbers.

    Which dogs are shown

    A dog needs a place in the picture, and a place comes from its links to relatives. dogs are shown: every dog connected through parents or offspring to the main pedigree, and with a birth year (recorded or estimated). Left out are small separate pedigrees (a dog with no known relatives, or a tiny family) and dogs with no date and no dated relatives, about % of the file.

    The layout

    Every sire and dam link is an edge in one network. A force-directed layout (DrL, from the igraph library) was run once, ahead of time: linked dogs pull together and unlinked dogs push apart. Dogs with many shared ancestors end up near each other, so distance is the only thing that means anything. The axes and the shape of the whole do not. The threads are the parent links themselves.

    Birth years

    About % of dogs have a recorded birth year. For the rest, the year is estimated from relatives: a dog's parents are placed about 4 years before it, and its offspring about 4 years after, repeated until no more dogs can be dated. Estimated years are marked in the tooltip. They are good to a few years at best, so treat a single estimated dog's date loosely and the overall shape with more trust.

    The time-lapse

    A dog appears when the clock reaches its birth year. The clock runs partly by calendar and partly by the number of dogs born, so the thin early decades are not slow and the busy ones are not rushed. The records thin out after about . Later years hold only our own litters and their ancestors, shown in blue on the timeline.

    Coat color

    The export records coat as free text. We group it by keyword into fawn or brindle, blue, harlequin, merle or mantle, black, and other. About % of dogs have no coat recorded at all, mostly in the 1990s, and appear as “Not recorded”.

    Inbreeding

    Inbreeding is the coefficient of inbreeding computed from the pedigree out to 10 generations: the chance that a dog inherited the same ancestral gene copy through both parents. Ancestors beyond 10 generations are treated as unrelated. Early dogs have shorter pedigrees, so their values are lower than their true inbreeding. The figure in the corner is the average for dogs born within two years of the clock.

    Tracing a dog

    Clicking a dog follows its parent links upward to every known ancestor and downward to every known descendant. Generations are counted along the shortest route. Descendants only include dogs that are in the file, which stops being complete around 2020.

    Kennels

    The export has no kennel field, so a kennel is read from registered names: the first word of a name, or the word after “of”, “von”, “de” and similar near the end. To keep dog call names out, the dogs carrying a name must be related to each other: at least half of them have a parent, an offspring, or a littermate or half-sibling that carries it too, because a kennel breeds its own dogs, while dogs that merely share a call name usually do not. Every kennel-like name on at least 25 dogs gets a button, about in all, and names on at least 5 dogs can be found by search. Names are grouped by the median birth year of the dogs that carry them. Our own kennels and Leggo, a friend’s, are listed first. The 7Sisters group is picked by registration, since our dogs’ names mostly come from other kennels’ affixes.

    Limits. Matching is by name only. Different breeders who use the same word are merged, and spelling variants of one kennel are split. A breeder with no affix is missed, and a few names that are really a breeder’s own dog line, not a kennel, can pass. Counts of kennels are therefore estimates: roughly 6,000 names appear on three or more dogs, of which a few thousand are real kennels with a lasting presence. “Descendants in the file” is how many dogs have a kennel’s dog somewhere in their pedigree, including the kennel’s own dogs.
